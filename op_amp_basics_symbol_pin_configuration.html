1. Introduction and learning objectives

An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a high-gain differential voltage amplifier. It senses the difference between two input voltages and produces an output voltage. With suitable external components and negative feedback, it can perform amplification, filtering, buffering, summing, integration and many other functions.

What you will learn

  • Read the op-amp symbol.
  • Identify LM741 pins.
  • Understand supply pins and input polarity.
  • Apply the ideal op-amp rules.
  • Recognise practical limitations.

Prerequisites

  • Voltage and current basics.
  • Ohm’s law and resistors.
  • Ground/reference voltage.
  • Basic DC and AC signals.

Key terms

  • Non-inverting (+): input whose increase tends to increase output.
  • Inverting (−): input whose increase tends to decrease output.
  • Feedback: returning part of output to an input.
  • Saturation: output reaches its voltage limit.
Core idea: An op-amp amplifies the difference, not either input alone. Its open-loop gain is so large that practical linear circuits usually use negative feedback to set a predictable gain.

Where op-amps matter

Sensor signal conditioning, audio preamplifiers, active filters, data-acquisition circuits, precision rectifiers, comparators (with important caveats), instrumentation front ends and analog control systems.

2. Op-amp circuit symbol

How to read the symbol

  • + input: a rise in this input relative to the − input tends to drive the output positive.
  • − input: a rise in this input relative to the + input tends to drive the output negative.
  • Output: the amplified response appears here.
  • +V and −V supplies: power the internal circuitry; they are not signal inputs.
Important: The triangle points toward the output. The plus/minus signs indicate input polarity, not the supply terminals.

Conceptual differential relationship

Vd = V+ − V−
Vout ≈ AOL(V+ − V−)

Here, V+ and V− are input voltages in volts (V), and AOL is the dimensionless open-loop voltage gain. This simple equation applies only while the output remains within its operating limits; in open loop, even a tiny input difference often drives the output into saturation.

Package orientation tip: For a standard DIP-8 viewed from above, locate the notch or pin-1 marker. Pin numbers run down the left side from 1 to 4, then up the right side from 5 to 8. Confirm the exact package drawing in the manufacturer datasheet before wiring.

Typical lab supply and wiring cautions

  • The classic LM741 is commonly used with dual supplies such as +15 V at pin 7 and −15 V at pin 4, with circuit ground as the 0 V reference. Always check the exact manufacturer's recommended supply range.
  • Do not connect the supply rails to pins 2 or 3. Those are signal inputs.
  • LM741 is an older design and is generally not suitable for single-supply, low-voltage, rail-to-rail applications. Its input common-mode range and output swing do not reach the rails.
  • Use supply decoupling capacitors close to the IC in practical circuits, and avoid shorting the output to a supply rail.

4. Working principle — step by step

  1. The circuit applies voltages to the + and − input terminals.
  2. The op-amp senses the differential input, Vd = V+ − V−.
  3. Its large internal gain tries to create a large output change in the polarity indicated by the differential input.
  4. Without feedback, the output usually moves close to one of its saturation limits.
  5. With negative feedback, the output feeds back in a way that reduces the input difference. In linear operation, the difference becomes very small.

Negative feedback and the “virtual short”

For an ideal op-amp operating linearly with negative feedback, the useful approximations are:

V+ ≈ V−    (virtual short)
I+ ≈ I− ≈ 0 A    (very high input resistance)

“Virtual short” means the input voltages are nearly equal; the two pins are not physically shorted. These rules do not apply automatically when the output is saturated, when feedback is positive, or when the circuit is unstable.

5. Ideal op-amp vs practical op-amp

ParameterIdeal assumptionPractical reality
Open-loop gainInfiniteVery high but finite; varies with frequency.
Input resistanceInfinite; no input currentHigh but finite; input bias currents exist.
Output resistanceZeroSmall but non-zero.
BandwidthInfiniteLimited; gain generally falls with frequency.
Input offset voltageZeroSmall differential voltage may be needed for zero output.
Slew rateInfiniteMaximum output-voltage rate is limited.
Output swingUnlimitedLimited by supply rails and device design.
CMRRInfiniteFinite; common-mode signals are not rejected perfectly.
Common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR): measures rejection of signals common to both inputs. In decibels, CMRR = 20 log10(Ad/Ac), where Ad is differential gain and Ac is common-mode gain.

Useful amplifier configurations

Inverting

Av = −Rf/Rin

Output is inverted by 180° in the passband; input signal enters the − node through Rin.

Non-inverting

Av = 1 + Rf/Rg

Output is in phase with the input; input signal is applied to the + terminal.

Voltage follower

Av ≈ 1

Output is connected to the − input. Useful as a buffer because input resistance is high and output resistance is low.

These closed-loop gain equations assume negative feedback, ideal-op-amp approximations, and operation within the device's bandwidth, input range and output swing.

6. Solved examples

Example 1 — differential input

Question: If V+ = 2.010 V and V− = 2.000 V, find Vd.

Formula: Vd = V+ − V−

Substitution: 2.010 V − 2.000 V = 0.010 V

Answer: Vd = +10 mV. The positive sign means the non-inverting input is higher. In open loop, even this small difference may drive the output toward positive saturation.

Example 2 — inverting amplifier gain

Given: Rin = 10 kΩ, Rf = 100 kΩ, Vin = 0.20 V. Assume an ideal op-amp with sufficient supply headroom.

Gain: Av = −Rf/Rin = −100/10 = −10.

Output: Vout = AvVin = −10 × 0.20 V = −2.0 V.

Answer: −2.0 V. The minus sign means the output has opposite polarity (180° phase inversion for a sinusoid in the operating band).

Example 3 — GATE-style non-inverting gain

Given: Rf = 18 kΩ and Rg = 2 kΩ. The + input receives 0.25 V. Find ideal closed-loop gain and output.

Av = 1 + Rf/Rg = 1 + 18/2 = 10.

Vout = 10 × 0.25 V = 2.5 V.

Answer: gain = 10 V/V; output = +2.5 V. This assumes the real output can swing to +2.5 V and the input common-mode voltage is allowed. Check the supply and device datasheet before a real build.

7. GATE-style practice questions

These are original practice questions, not claimed to be actual previous-year GATE questions. Expand each item to reveal the answer and reasoning.

Easy 1. An op-amp amplifies which quantity?

The differential input voltage V+ − V−. Its output depends on the difference, not simply the sum of the input voltages.

Easy 2. Which LM741 pin is the non-inverting input?

Pin 3. Pin 2 is the inverting input.

Easy 3. Which LM741 pin is the output?

Pin 6. Pins 7 and 4 are the positive and negative supply rails.

Moderate 4. For V+ = 1.2 V and V− = 1.1 V, find Vd.

Vd = 1.2 − 1.1 = +0.1 V.

Moderate 5. An inverting amplifier has Rf = 47 kΩ and Rin = 4.7 kΩ. Find gain.

Av = −Rf/Rin = −47/4.7 = −10 V/V.

Moderate 6. Why does a tiny input difference often saturate an open-loop op-amp?

Because the open-loop gain is extremely high and the output is bounded by its supply and output-stage limits.

Moderate 7. What does the virtual-short approximation mean?

Under stable negative feedback and linear operation, V+ ≈ V−. It does not mean the pins are physically connected.

Moderate 8. An ideal non-inverting amplifier has Rf = 9 kΩ and Rg = 1 kΩ. Find gain.

Av = 1 + 9/1 = 10 V/V.

Difficult 9. If Ad = 100,000 and Ac = 10, find CMRR in dB.

CMRR = 20 log10(Ad/Ac) = 20 log10(10,000) = 80 dB.

Difficult 10. Why can an LM741 fail to produce the expected output on a 0–5 V supply?

It is not a rail-to-rail, low-voltage op-amp. Its allowed input common-mode range and output swing require headroom from the rails; use a suitable single-supply device instead.

8. Core electronics interview questions

Select a question to expand a concise interview-ready answer.

1. What is an operational amplifier?

A high-gain differential voltage amplifier that ideally amplifies the difference between its non-inverting and inverting inputs.

2. Why is the input called non-inverting?

In a standard non-inverting closed-loop configuration, the output follows the input's polarity and phase. A positive input change produces a positive output change.

3. Why is negative feedback used?

It sets predictable closed-loop gain, improves linearity and typically increases usable bandwidth while reducing sensitivity to device-gain variation.

4. What is a virtual ground?

A node held near 0 V by negative feedback even though it is not directly connected to ground. This approximation applies only when the feedback loop is functioning linearly.

5. What are the LM741 supply pins?

Pin 7 is V+ and pin 4 is V−. The exact supply voltages must follow the specific datasheet and circuit requirements.

6. What is input offset voltage?

The small differential DC voltage required between the inputs to make the output zero. It can create output error, especially at high closed-loop gain.

7. Define slew rate.

The maximum rate of change of output voltage, usually expressed in V/µs. If the required output changes faster, the waveform distorts.

8. What is CMRR and why does it matter?

Common-mode rejection ratio indicates how well an op-amp rejects signals shared by both inputs. High CMRR is valuable for small sensor signals in noisy environments.

9. The output is stuck near a rail. What would you check?

Check supply polarity and magnitude, pinout, input common-mode range, feedback wiring, input differential voltage, load, and whether the requested output exceeds the allowed swing.

10. Why should the LM741 not be the default choice for a 3.3 V microcontroller sensor?

Its supply and input/output ranges are unsuitable for many low-voltage single-supply systems. Choose a modern op-amp with the required supply range, common-mode range, output swing, bandwidth, noise and input offset.

Industry perspective

In product development, the symbol and pinout are only the start. Engineers also verify supply range, input common-mode range, output swing under load, gain-bandwidth product, slew rate, noise, offset, stability with capacitive loads, package pin numbering and PCB decoupling. Simulation helps, but the chosen model and real datasheet must agree.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • High differential gain.
  • Useful input impedance and output drive in many configurations.
  • One basic building block supports many analog functions.
  • Negative feedback enables predictable closed-loop behaviour.

Limitations

  • Finite bandwidth and slew rate.
  • Limited input/output voltage ranges.
  • Offset voltage, bias current and noise.
  • Stability and load-driving constraints.
  • LM741 is not ideal for modern low-voltage systems.

Final 30-second recap

An op-amp amplifies V+ − V−. The + terminal is non-inverting and the − terminal is inverting. For the LM741: pin 2 = − input, pin 3 = + input, pin 4 = V−, pin 6 = output, pin 7 = V+, pins 1 and 5 = offset null, pin 8 = NC. Negative feedback makes linear amplifier circuits predictable, but practical supply, bandwidth, slew-rate and output-swing limits always matter.
